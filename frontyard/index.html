THE HIGH TOWER DISTRICT

Sermon · September 6, 2026

A Front Yard Is Not a Battlefield

A cleaned word, offered in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, after a shooting on West Berkeley Avenue.

Brothers and sisters, a man was shot in front of his own house. That is the first sentence. Everything else is commentary. ABC30 Action News reported that on Saturday afternoon, about 2 p.m., on West Berkeley Avenue near Clinton and Cornelia in West Central Fresno, two people estimated to be fifteen to seventeen years old approached a man in his twenties standing in front of his home. There was a verbal fight. One of them pulled a gun and opened fire. The man was hit in the legs and the right hand. He was taken to the hospital with serious injuries and is expected to live. The two fled on foot in dark clothing. Detectives are asking whether the shooting was gang related. That is the public record. That is what happened in a front yard that should have been ordinary.

I wrote a comment under that story. The comment was raw. It was angry. It used words I will not repeat from this pulpit. I am not ashamed of the heat. Heat is what a city feels when a man can be shot on the grass in front of his door in daylight. But heat is not the same as truth, and the Lord Jesus Christ does not need our profanity to tell the truth. So I am bringing the same comment back, cleaned, and I am setting it under Scripture. The New Living Translation will speak. I will only point.

The cleaned word

Here is the comment without the cursing, without the sneer that makes a neighbor stop listening.

So they are teenagers. Does that make it acceptable? If they had also been homeless, some of our public talk would have turned toward food and caution and almost nothing toward the man bleeding in his own yard. Fresno has a habit of soft reporting when the facts are hard. When two young people shoot a man in his front yard, those are not children at play. That is criminal violence. Nobody should be shot in a front yard by anyone, of any age. Age does not wash the act. A load of polite language will not either.

That is the whole argument. Age is not an altar. Poverty is not an altar. A headline that leads with “teenagers” can become a kind of incense, and the incense can hide the blood. The prophet already named that habit.

“What sorrow for those who say that evil is good and good is evil, that dark is light and light is dark, that bitter is sweet and sweet is bitter.” (Isaiah 5:20, New Living Translation)

Isaiah 5:20 is not a slogan for internet fights. It is a warning to a city that has learned to rename things. If we call a shooting “youth” long enough, we start to treat the victim as an inconvenience in a coming-of-age story. That is darkness calling itself light. The man on West Berkeley Avenue was not a plot point. He was a neighbor with legs and a right hand, standing where a man is allowed to stand.

What the word “teenager” is being asked to do

A teenager is a human being between childhood and adulthood. The word is accurate as a range of years. It is not a moral category. Fifteen can hold a gun. Seventeen can pull a trigger. The law of California treats juveniles differently from adults in court, and that is a civic decision about formation and hope. The law of God does not treat the wound differently because the shooter has not yet turned eighteen. Abel’s blood still cried from the ground. Cain was not given a lighter sentence in Genesis because he was young in the world. The first murder in Scripture is a family murder, and the Lord asked a simple question: “Where is your brother?” He did not ask, “How old were you when you raised your hand?”

Proverbs speaks to the young with no romance about their capacity for blood.

“My child, if sinners entice you, turn your back on them! They may say, ‘Come and join us. Let’s hide and kill someone! Just for fun, let’s ambush the innocent! Let’s swallow them alive, like the grave; let’s swallow them whole, like those who go down to the pit of death. Think of the great things we’ll get! We’ll fill our houses with all the stuff we take. Come, throw in your lot with us; we’ll all share the loot.’ My child, don’t go along with them! Stay far from their paths. They rush to commit evil deeds. They hurry to commit murder.” (Proverbs 1:10-16, New Living Translation)

That passage is older than Fresno, older than California, older than the phrase “gang related.” It already knew the pitch: come with us, it will be shared, it will be fun, the innocent are available. The father in Proverbs does not say the child is incapable of the walk. He says do not take the walk. Capability is assumed. Warning is the love.

So when a newsroom leads with “two teenagers,” the words can be factual and still do extra work. They can recruit pity before they recruit justice. Pity is not a sin. Pity for a fifteen-year-old who has already learned to fire on a man in a yard is still pity that must stand next to the man on the ground. If pity only faces the shooter, it has chosen a side and called the choice compassion.

The front yard as holy ordinary ground

A house is not only lumber and stucco. In the older language of Scripture, a household is a trust. A threshold is a line. When violence crosses that line in daylight, the city is being taught that no line holds. That lesson travels. It travels from West Berkeley to every porch that heard the shots or heard the story. It travels into the High Tower District, into every block where people still believe a front yard is a place to stand without calculating cover.

The psalmist prayed for the weak as if their ground mattered.

“Give justice to the poor and the orphan; uphold the rights of the oppressed and the destitute. Rescue the poor and helpless; deliver them from the grasp of evil people.” (Psalm 82:3-4, New Living Translation)

A man in his twenties with gunshot wounds in his own yard is not an abstraction of “the poor,” and he may not be poor at all. He is, in that hour, helpless in the exact sense the psalm names. He is in the grasp of people who chose a gun over a departure. Rescue, in Psalm 82, is not a mood. It is an assignment given to those who still have breath and office and a voice.

That is why the original comment refused the lullaby. Softness toward the category “teenager” can become hardness toward the man who was supposed to be safe on his own dirt. The Lord Jesus Christ never taught us to invert mercy that way. He ate with sinners. He also said the tree is known by its fruit. A gun in a front yard is fruit. We are allowed to say so.

Civil authority is not optional piety

Some religious talk treats the police and the courts as a necessary evil that spiritual people should mostly ignore. Paul did not write that letter.

“For the authorities do not strike fear in people who are doing right, but in those who are doing wrong. Would you like to live without fear of the authorities? Do what is right, and they will honor you. The authorities are God’s servants, sent for your good. But if you are doing wrong, of course you should be afraid, for they have the power to punish you. They are God’s servants, sent for the very purpose of punishing those who do what is wrong.” (Romans 13:3-4, New Living Translation)

This is not a blank check for every officer or every headline. It is a refusal to pretend that a city can live by slogans while men are shot at 2 p.m. on a Saturday. Detectives asking whether a shooting is gang related are doing the work Romans 13 describes. Neighbors who know a name and stay silent are doing the opposite of Proverbs 24.

“Rescue those who are unjustly sentenced to die; save them as they stagger to their death. Don’t excuse yourself by saying, ‘Look, we didn’t know.’ For God understands all hearts, and he sees you. He who guards your soul knows you knew. He will repay all people as their actions deserve.” (Proverbs 24:11-12, New Living Translation)

The victim on West Berkeley Avenue was not sentenced by a court. He was sentenced in a yard by a gun. The proverb still fits, because the excuse is the same: we did not know, we did not see, we do not want the trouble of knowing. God is not mocked by that shrug. He understands all hearts. He knows when a city knew.

Ephesians makes the same demand in the church’s own language.

“Take no part in the worthless deeds of evil and darkness; instead, expose them.” (Ephesians 5:11, New Living Translation)

Expose them. Not decorate them. Not give them a softer noun and walk away. Exposure is a Christian act. It is also a neighbor act. If you have information, the Fresno Police Department has asked for it. That sentence belongs in a sermon because love of neighbor is not only soup. Sometimes love of neighbor is a phone call that ends a hunt.

Do not make children into weapons, and do not make weapons into children

The Lord Jesus Christ spoke with a severity that modern religious talk often edits out.

“But if you cause one of these little ones who trusts in me to fall into sin, it would be better for you to have a large millstone tied around your neck and be drowned in the depths of the sea.” (Matthew 18:6, New Living Translation)

Matthew 18:6 is first about those who prey on the trusting young. It also judges the adults who hand a boy a worldview in which a front yard is a place to settle a verbal fight with bullets. Someone taught those two a story about power. Someone failed to teach them another story. The millstone is not only for the hand that pulls the trigger. It is for the hand that formed the hand.

At the same time, we must not perform the opposite lie: that a fifteen-year-old with a gun is still, in the moment of firing, only a child in need of a snack. That is the exact swap Isaiah condemned. It is bitter called sweet. A city that will not tell a young shooter the truth will not be able to tell him the gospel either, because the gospel begins with the fact that we have sinned. If the act is only a social symptom, there is nothing to confess. If there is nothing to confess, the cross is decoration.

Paul described the climate of a late age without flattering it.

“You should know this, Timothy, that in the last days there will be very difficult times. For people will love only themselves and their money. They will be boastful and proud, scoffing at God, disobedient to their parents, and ungrateful. They will consider nothing sacred. They will be unloving and unforgiving; they will slander others and have no self-control. They will be cruel and hate what is good. They will betray their friends, be reckless, be puffed up with pride, and love pleasure rather than God. They will act religious, but they will reject the power that could make them godly. Stay away from people like that!” (2 Timothy 3:1-5, New Living Translation)

“They will consider nothing sacred.” A front yard is sacred in the ordinary sense: it is a place a household is given to keep. Recklessness that spends a man’s legs and hand to win a talk on the sidewalk is the cruelty Paul named. Acting religious while rejecting the power that could make a person godly is the other half of the danger, and it lives in churches as much as it lives in crews. A sermon that only scolds the street and never the sanctuary is a partial sermon.

The Lord does not delight in their death

If I stop at anger I have only rewritten the Facebook comment with better manners. The gospel does not let us stop there. The same God who names the blood also names the door back.

“As surely as I live, says the Sovereign Lord, I take no pleasure in the death of wicked people. I only want them to turn from their wicked ways so they can live. Turn! Turn from your wickedness, O people of Israel! Why should you die?” (Ezekiel 33:11, New Living Translation)

That word is for the two who ran. It is for whoever put the gun in the story. It is for the man who survived and will have to walk with the memory in his legs. It is for a city that has grown used to Saturday shootings. The Sovereign Lord does not collect corpses for sport. He commands a turn. A turn is not a press conference. A turn is not a softer headline. A turn is leaving the path in Proverbs 1 and staying off it when the next voice says come with us.

Jesus said the same thing without decoration.

“Not at all! And you will perish, too, unless you repent of your sins and turn to God.” (Luke 13:3, New Living Translation)

Repentance is not reserved for people over eighteen. A young person can turn. An adult who has spent years explaining violence as weather can turn. A commentator who used cruel words because the cruelty of the act felt larger than vocabulary can turn, and I include myself. Cleaning the comment is part of that turn. The heat stays. The filth goes. The Lord Jesus Christ is not honored by a mouth that cannot tell the difference.

John explains why people prefer the dark headline to the true one.

“And the judgment is based on this fact: God’s light came into the world, but people loved the darkness more than the light, for their actions were evil. All who do evil hate the light and refuse to go near it for fear their sins will be exposed.” (John 3:19-20, New Living Translation)

Exposure is what the light does. That is why a city argues about nouns. “Teenagers” can be a lamp with a cloth over it. “Suspects in a shooting” is less comfortable and more true. Truth is not cruelty. Darkness is cruelty with good lighting.

What we are required to do now

Micah already reduced the assignment to a size a neighborhood can carry.

“No, O people, the Lord has told you what is good, and this is what he requires of you: to do what is right, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God.” (Micah 6:8, New Living Translation)

Do what is right. That includes telling the Fresno Police what you know, if you know it. Love mercy. That includes refusing to celebrate anyone’s damnation, including two young people who may still be found and still be able to tell the truth in a courtroom and then in prayer. Walk humbly with your God. That includes not pretending that our block is immune because the address was West Berkeley and not our own street.

James will not let a sermon end as vapor.

“But don’t just listen to God’s word. You must do what it says. Otherwise, you are only fooling yourselves.” (James 1:22, New Living Translation)

Doing the word, this week, looks like ordinary courage. Check on the people you know near Clinton and Cornelia. Do not traffic in rumors that name the wrong house. Do not use the victim as a mascot for your politics. Do not use the suspects as a mascot for your despair. Pray for the man in the hospital by the only name we have: a neighbor. Pray for the two who fled, not as celebrities of violence, but as souls who must be stopped and must be saved, in that order if the city is to remain a city. Pray for the officers who have to treat a yard as a scene. Pray for the parents who either lost control of a child or never had it. Pray for the mouths of this city, including the one that wrote the first comment, that we would stop blessing what God has called bitter.

Jesus is not a mascot for toughness and He is not a mascot for softness. He is the one who said both “go and sin no more” and “Father, forgive them.” Those sentences are not enemies. Forgiveness that refuses to name the sin is flattery. Naming the sin that refuses the possibility of a turn is despair. The church is not authorized to live in either ditch.

A last word to the language

I return to the cleaned comment so no one can say the sermon wandered off.

They were reported as teenagers. That does not make the shooting acceptable. If the only public instinct we have for young people in crisis is food and a warning to be careful, we have no instinct left for the man who was not careful enough to avoid a gun he did not bring to his own door. Fresno does not need more backward reporting. It needs reporting that can hold two facts in one sentence: they were young, and they are accused of shooting a man in his front yard. Those facts do not cancel. The second fact is the one that makes the first fact tragic instead of cute.

Those who fire on a neighbor in a yard have taken up a work the Bible does not call youth. It calls it wickedness. The Lord Jesus Christ can still reach a wicked person. He reached Paul. He reached the thief on the cross. He can reach a fifteen-year-old in dark clothes running down a Fresno street. Reaching him is not the same as lying about what he did.

Paul told the church in Rome how to dress after the argument is over.

“Instead, clothe yourself with the presence of the Lord Jesus Christ. And don’t let yourself think about ways to indulge your evil desires.” (Romans 13:14, New Living Translation)

That is the end of the sermon and the beginning of the week. Clothe yourself with Christ. Do not indulge the desire to enjoy another person’s fall. Do not indulge the desire to rename blood. A front yard is not a battlefield unless we keep surrendering it. West Berkeley Avenue already paid part of that price on Saturday. The rest of the city does not have to add a tip.

In the name of the Father, and of the Son, the Lord Jesus Christ, and in the fear of Jehovah who sees every yard. Amen.
